# PRODUCT VISION

## The one-sentence version

Turn Vietnamese military history from something you read into something you can interrogate.

## The problem

Popular history gives you a result and a story: *Ngô Quyền planted stakes, the tide fell, the
fleet was destroyed, Vietnam became independent.* You remember the story. You learn almost
nothing about **why it worked**, what had to go right, or how narrow the margin was.

And a lot of what circulates is not solid. The famous troop figures for Bạch Đằng 938 have no
traceable attribution; one widely repeated ship-complement figure is internally inconsistent.
Confident numbers get copied between sources until they feel like facts.

## What we build instead

A simulation you can push on.

The player commands a historical battle, makes decisions under the same physical constraints the
real commanders faced, and watches the consequences. Then they change one variable and run it
again.

The goal is a specific moment of understanding: **"Oh — that is why the timing mattered."**

Not "Vietnam won because they were clever." Rather: *a ship drawing 1.5 m needs 1.7 m of water to
clear an obstruction; the tide falls roughly 3 m over about twelve hours; therefore a fleet that
delays half an hour does not get out.* That is a mechanism, and a mechanism can be explored.

## Principles

### 1. Never invent history and present it as fact

The hardest rule and the most important one. Where sources are unreliable, the game says so.
Where we invented a number because the simulation needed one, it is labelled a gameplay
assumption. Where sources conflict, the conflict is shown rather than silently resolved.

This costs us convenience constantly, and it is the whole point. A history game that fabricates
confidently is worse than no history game.

### 2. Let the evidence decide what the game is about

The first battle is Bạch Đằng 1288 rather than the more famous 938 precisely because 1288 is
where the decisive mechanic rests on excavated, radiocarbon-dated physical evidence. The
simulation leans hardest on what we actually know.

The corollary: the battle is decided by *timing against the tide* (measurable) rather than by
*troop counts* (unreliable). Gameplay weight follows evidence weight.

### 3. Explain, do not just adjudicate

"You won" is not interesting. "You won because the ebb caught four squadrons above the
obstructions, and here is the log" is interesting. Every post-battle finding is marked OBSERVED,
INFERRED or SPECULATIVE, and observed findings are derived from the event log rather than
narrated.

### 4. What-if is a simulation, never a claim

Removing the stakes and watching eight ships escape instead of four tells you something real
about the *model*. It tells you nothing certain about 1288. The product must never blur that
line, and its language is written to avoid doing so.

### 5. Respect

Historical figures are real people, treated in context. Commander ratings exist because the
simulation needs numbers; they are game statistics and are never presented as historical
judgements of anyone's competence.

## Who this is for

- Someone curious about Vietnamese history who wants more than a summary
- Students and teachers who want a mechanism, not a date to memorise
- Strategy players who like systems that are about something real
- Anyone who has wondered how much of what they were taught is actually attested

## What success looks like

A player finishes a battle, reads why it went the way it did, tries a what-if, sees the outcome
change — and then goes and looks up the real history because they now have a question they
actually want answered.

If the game makes someone more curious and more sceptical at the same time, it has worked.

## What this is not

- Not a documentary. It is a model, and models are wrong in known ways.
- Not a claim to settle historical debates. It records them.
- Not nationalist. Honouring people through gameplay is fine; inventing history to flatter them
  is not.
- Not a general RTS with a Vietnamese skin. The historical layer is the product.
